When clothing travels, does responsibility travel too?

A digital product passport can describe a garment. But circularity depends on whether accurate, usable information reaches the people who sort, repair and recover it—and whether policy supports the systems working beyond the EU.

Why this connects to Circle Economy

One garment. Three research questions.

Measure material flows

Circle Economy’s Circularity Gap Report for Textiles follows materials from production to post-consumer management. This case asks what product-level information could make those flows more visible.

Understand the whole value chain

Used clothing crosses borders. A circular transition needs to account for the workers, businesses and communities that enable sorting, repair, resale and recovery in trading-partner countries.

Connect evidence to policy

Circle Economy’s textile passport recommendations focus on access, traceability, reliable data and implementation support. The stages below turn those recommendations into practical decision points.

Actor 01 · downstream business

Repair & reuse business

A repairer needs to identify a garment and understand its construction before recommending a repair or resale route.

Decision to support

Can this item be repaired, and what is the most suitable next use?

Information needed

Clear material composition, care instructions and relevant repair guidance that can be accessed without specialist software.

Policy design test

Can a small repair business access and use the information at low cost, in the language and format it needs?

Red flag to watch for

The passport exists, but repairers cannot find or understand the fields they need.

The point I want to make

A passport is not circularity by itself.

It can help people make circular decisions only when its information is reliable, accessible to the actors who need it, and supported by workable systems for collecting and checking data.

And when clothing moves across borders, policy needs to consider where the benefits, implementation costs and end-of-life responsibilities land—not only what happens in the market where the product was first sold.

Research question: how can textile policy and product data support circularity across borders without shifting costs down the chain?
What this concept demonstrates

From a product tool

My current work at DAMEN involves structuring product and material information for a ship-cabin passport. Here, I carry that practical data question into textiles—a sector central to Circle Economy’s research—and connect it to material-flow analysis, global value chains and policy design.
